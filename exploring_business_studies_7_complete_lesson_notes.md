# Chapter 1: Business Fundamentals

## 1.1 Basic Economic Concepts
* **Textbook Reference:** Pages 1–8

### 1. Core Economic Problem: Scarcity
* **Definition of Economics:** The study of resources, their utilization, and how decisions are made by individuals, businesses, and governments in an effort to satisfy **unlimited wants** with **limited (scarce) resources**.
* **Scarcity:** Arises because human wants are infinite, but the productive resources available to satisfy those wants are finite. Scarcity affects every individual, firm, and national economy.
* **The Cycle of Desires:** When one need or want is fulfilled, a new desire arises, creating a continuous demand for resources.

### 2. Needs vs. Wants
* **Needs:**
  * **Definition:** Basic physical necessities essential for human survival. Without satisfying these, life cannot be sustained.
  * **Hierarchy:** Needs must always be satisfied prior to wants.
  * **Core Examples:** Safe drinking water, nutritious food, protective clothing, secure shelter.
* **Wants:**
  * **Definition:** Desires for goods or services that an individual would like to possess but which are not essential for survival; an individual can live without them.
  * **Nature:** Unlimited and ever-expanding.
  * **Core Examples:** Video games, branded luxury goods, overseas leisure vacations, high-end smartphones.

### 3. Classification of Economic Resources
Resources are the inputs used to produce outputs (goods and services). They are divided into three fundamental categories:
1. **Natural Resources:**
   * Raw materials provided directly by nature/earth.
   * Solid, liquid, or gas forms (water, fertile soil, sand, rocks, forests, fish, fossil fuels, minerals).
   * **Sub-classifications:**
     * *Renewable Resources:* Can be naturally replenished within a reasonable human timeframe or are continuously available (e.g., solar energy, wind, sustainably managed trees, fresh water).
     * *Non-renewable Resources:* Resources that take geological eras to form or cannot be replaced once extracted and consumed (e.g., crude oil, coal, minerals, natural gas).
2. **Human Resources (Labour):**
   * Human effort, time, physical labor, and intellect directed toward the production of goods and services.
3. **Manufactured / Capital Resources:**
   * Tools, machinery, factories, roads, and equipment constructed by humans to assist future production.

### 4. Choice and Opportunity Cost
* **Choice:** The act of selecting between two or more competing alternatives. Because productive resources are scarce, choices must be made daily by consumers, businesses, and governments.
* **Opportunity Cost:** The cost of the next best alternative foregone when making a choice.
  * It is not merely the monetary expense paid; it represents the real sacrifice of goods, services, or time that could have been enjoyed instead.
* **Decision-Making Principle:** Economic agents seek to maximize overall utility and satisfaction when allocating limited resources among competing choices.

### 5. Consumption, Markets, Demand, and Supply
* **Consumption:** The using up of goods and services to satisfy human needs and wants.
* **Market:** Any physical or digital arrangement where buyers and sellers interact to negotiate and exchange goods and services.
* **Demand:** The quantity of a good or service that consumers are willing and able to purchase at various price levels over a given time period.
* **Supply:** The quantity of a good or service that producers are willing and able to offer for sale at various price levels over a given time period.

* **Owners / Shareholders:** Seek long-term capital growth, return on investment, and profit maximization.

# Chapter 2: Basic Financial Accounting

## 2.1 The Role of Accounting and Book-keeping
* **Textbook Reference:** Pages 17–28

### 1. Book-keeping vs. Accounting
* **Book-keeping:** The systematic, daily recording of all financial transactions of a business in an orderly, verifiable manner.
* **Accounting:** The broader discipline that involves summarizing, analyzing, interpreting, and communicating financial records to allow owners, managers, and external stakeholders to make informed economic decisions.

### 2. Assets, Liabilities, and Owner's Equity
* **Assets:** Resources owned by the business (or debts owed to the business by customers) that hold quantifiable monetary value.
  * *Tangible Assets:* Physical items that can be seen and touched (vehicles, office desks, factory equipment).
  * *Intangible Assets:* Non-physical rights and value (education, software licenses, patents, business brand reputation).
  * *Non-Current Assets:* Long-term assets acquired for continuing business operations rather than resale, with an expected useful life exceeding one operating year (premises, machinery, delivery speedboats).
  * *Current Assets:* Short-term liquid assets that can be converted into cash within twelve months (inventories, trade receivables, bank balance, petty cash).
* **Liabilities:** Financial debts and obligations owed by the business to outside third parties.
  * *Current Liabilities:* Debts due for settlement within twelve months (trade payables, short-term overdrafts).
  * *Non-Current Liabilities:* Long-term loans or debt instruments repayable over a multi-year timeframe (bank loans, long-term mortgages).
* **Owner's Equity (Capital):** The owner's financial stake in the business; the residual interest in the business's assets after deducting all liabilities:
  $$\text{Owner's Equity} = \text{Total Assets} - \text{Total Liabilities}$$

### 3. The Fundamental Accounting Equation
Every double-entry bookkeeping system rests upon the core equation:
$$\text{Assets} = \text{Capital} + \text{Liabilities}$$
or expressed as:
$$\text{Assets} - \text{Liabilities} = \text{Owner's Equity}$$

## 2.2 Double-Entry Principles & Ledgers
* **Textbook Reference:** Pages 29–48

### 1. The Duality Principle
Every financial transaction affects at least two accounts in opposing, balancing entries: for every **Debit** entry, there must be a matching **Credit** entry of equal value.

```
       DEBIT (Left Side)              CREDIT (Right Side)
+------------------------------+-------------------------------+
| * Increases in Assets        | * Decreases in Assets         |
| * Decreases in Liabilities   | * Increases in Liabilities    |
| * Increases in Expenses      | * Decreases in Expenses       |
| * Decreases in Capital       | * Increases in Capital        |
| * Decreases in Revenues      | * Increases in Revenues       |
+------------------------------+-------------------------------+
```

## 2.3 Financial Statements of a Sole Trader
* **Textbook Reference:** Pages 49–64

Financial statements summarize the financial performance and standing of a firm at the end of an accounting period.

### 1. The Income Statement (Trading and Profit & Loss)
Measures the operating performance and profitability achieved over a defined period.

$$\text{Gross Profit} = \text{Net Revenue} - \text{Cost of Goods Sold (COGS)}$$

$$\text{COGS} = \text{Opening Inventory} + \text{Purchases} + \text{Carriage Inwards} - \text{Closing Inventory}$$

$$\text{Net Profit / (Loss)} = \text{Gross Profit} + \text{Other Income} - \text{Total Operating Expenses}$$

### 2. The Balance Sheet (Statement of Financial Position)
A structured snapshot of what a business owns, owes, and the owner's remaining equity as of a specific calendar date.

# Chapter 3: Entrepreneurship & Production

## 3.1 Allocation of Resources & The Three Economic Questions
* **Textbook Reference:** Pages 65–70

Because all resources are scarce, an economy must resolve three foundational questions:
1. **What to produce?** Deciding which goods and services to make, and in what quantities, taking into account opportunity costs.
2. **How to produce?** Determining the combination of resources and technology to use—choosing between labor-intensive and capital-intensive methods.
3. **For whom to produce?** Determining how the goods and services produced will be shared and distributed among the population.

## 3.2 Economic Systems
* **Textbook Reference:** Pages 71–76

| System Type | Mechanism for Allocation | Ownership of Resources | Key Advantages | Key Disadvantages |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Market Economy** (Free Enterprise) | Price mechanism (forces of demand and supply without state intervention). | Purely private individuals and corporate entities. | High efficiency, consumer choice, strong innovation incentive. | Wealth inequality, failure to provide public goods, negative externalities. |
| **Planned Economy** (Command) | Central state directives, planning committees, quotas. | State / Government owned. | Focus on social welfare, universal employment, zero duplication. | Inflexibility, severe shortages, slow innovation, lack of consumer choice. |
| **Mixed Economy** | Both market forces and government interventions operate together. | Private sector alongside public state sector. | Combines private enterprise drive with public safety nets and infrastructure. | Bureaucratic regulations, taxation burdens, potential public sector debt. |

* **Context (Maldives):** Operates as a **Mixed Economy**, where private enterprises run the tourism, fishing, retail, and maritime transport sectors, while the government provides infrastructure, public utilities, education, and healthcare through public departments and state-owned enterprises (SOEs).

## 3.3 Methods and Sectors of Production
* **Textbook Reference:** Pages 77–88

### 1. Sectors of Production
* **Primary Sector:** Industries that extract raw natural materials directly from the earth, sea, or natural environment (tuna pole-and-line fishing, agriculture, mining, quarrying).
* **Secondary Sector:** Industries that process, manufacture, assemble, or refine primary sector raw materials into finished or semi-finished physical goods (tuna canning at Felivaru, boat building, garment manufacturing, construction).
* **Tertiary Sector:** Industries that provide commercial, professional, and personal services to consumers and businesses (resort tourism, sea transport, banking, telecommunications, retail shops).

### 2. Methods of Production
* **Job Production:**
  * One-off, unique products custom-made to customer specifications from start to finish.
  * *Examples:* Custom wooden safari boat construction, bespoke tailoring, architectural design.
* **Batch Production:**
  * A group of identical products produced together through each stage of the production process before moving on to another batch.
  * *Examples:* Bakery producing 200 loaves of wholemeal bread, followed by 300 pastries.
* **Flow (Mass) Production:**
  * Continuous, uninterrupted movement of uniform products along an automated assembly line.
  * *Examples:* Industrial fish canning line (e.g., Felivaru Tuna Canning Factory), automated beverage bottling plants.

### 3. Production Resource Intensity
* **Labour-Intensive Production:** Relies predominantly on human workers and manual labor rather than machinery. Common where labor is flexible, affordable, or artistic/custom work is needed.
* **Capital-Intensive Production:** Relies predominantly on machinery, industrial automation, and computerized systems rather than manual labor. Requires large capital investment, but achieves high speed, consistency, and scale.

## 3.4 Division of Labour, Productivity, and Diminishing Returns
* **Textbook Reference:** Pages 89–96

### 1. Division of Labour & Specialization
* **Definition:** Dividing a complex production process into separate, smaller tasks, with each individual worker specializing in a single task.
* **Advantages:**
  * Workers become faster and more skilled at their specialized job through repetition.
  * Saves time otherwise spent switching tools and stations.
  * Simplifies worker training.
* **Disadvantages:**
  * Extreme repetition leads to boredom, fatigue, and lower motivation.
  * Absenteeism at one workstation can disrupt the entire production line.

### 2. Productivity Measures
Productivity measures how efficiently inputs are converted into output:
$$\text{Productivity} = \frac{\text{Total Output}}{\text{Total Input}}$$

* **Total Product ($TP$):** The total quantity of output produced by a firm over a given period.
* **Average Product ($AP$):** Total product per unit of variable input:
  $$AP = \frac{TP}{\text{Labour Units}}$$
* **Marginal Product ($MP$):** The extra output produced by adding one more unit of variable input:
  $$MP = \frac{\Delta TP}{\Delta \text{Labour Units}}$$

### 3. The Law of Diminishing Returns
As equal additional units of a variable factor (such as labor) are added to a fixed factor of production (such as a fixed plot of land or factory building), a point will eventually be reached where the addition to total output (Marginal Product) begins to decline.

## 3.5 Marketing and Promotion
* **Textbook Reference:** Pages 97–104
* **Promotion:** Communication strategies designed to raise customer awareness of a brand or product, generate initial sales, and foster long-term brand loyalty.
* **Methods of Promotion:**
  * *Advertising:* Paid public announcements using print, social media, radio, and TV.
  * *Sales Promotion:* Short-term sales incentives (discounts, buy-one-get-one-free offers, loyalty points).
  * *Personal Selling:* Direct, verbal sales presentations by sales representatives.
  * *Public Relations (PR):* Building a positive public reputation through sponsorships, community programs, and press releases.

## 4.1 Forms of Business Ownership
* **Textbook Reference:** Pages 105–118

```

                                          |
          +-------------------------------+-------------------------------+
          |                                                               |
  UNINCORPORATED BUSINESSES                                     INCORPORATED BUSINESSES
(Unlimited Liability / No Separate Legal Entity)              (Limited Liability / Separate Legal Entity)
          |                                                               |
    +-----+-----+                                                   +-----+-----+
    |           |                                                   |           |
Sole Trader  Partnership                                    Private Limited  Public Limited
                                                               Company (Ltd)   Company (Plc)
```

### 1. Sole Proprietorship (Sole Trader)
* **Definition:** A business owned and operated by a single individual.
* **Liability:** **Unlimited Liability** — the owner is personally responsible for all business debts; personal assets (house, car, savings) can be claimed to pay off creditors.
* **Key Advantages:** Easy and inexpensive to establish; owner keeps 100% of profits; complete independence in decision-making; financial accounts remain private.
* **Key Disadvantages:** Unlimited liability; limited sources of expansion capital; heavy workload and personal pressure; lacks continuity (business legally ends if owner passes away).

### 2. Partnership
* **Definition:** A business structure formed and owned by two or more individuals (typically 2 to 20 partners) governed by a Partnership Agreement.
* **Liability:** Unlimited liability for general partners (shared joint responsibility).
* **Key Advantages:** More capital can be pooled; complementary skills and shared workload; shared financial risk.
* **Key Disadvantages:** Unlimited liability; potential for disagreements between partners; decisions made by one partner legally bind all others; profits must be shared.

### 3. Limited Companies (Incorporated Entities)
* **Separate Legal Entity:** The company has its own distinct legal identity separate from its shareholders. The company can own property, sign contracts, and be sued in its own name.
* **Limited Liability:** A shareholder's financial liability is strictly limited to the amount invested or agreed to be paid on their shares. Personal assets cannot be taken to pay business debts.
* **Private Limited Company (Ltd):**
  * Shares sold privately to family, friends, or trusted investors.
  * Shares cannot be offered or traded on a public stock exchange.
  * Retains privacy compared to public companies; protects ownership control.
* **Public Limited Company (Plc):**
  * Shares can be freely sold and traded by the general public on an official stock exchange.
  * Capable of raising substantial investment capital.
  * Subject to strict statutory reporting rules, audit disclosures, and risk of takeover.

## 4.2 Other Business Forms & Public Sector Enterprise
* **Textbook Reference:** Pages 119–130

### 1. Special Business Structures
* **Franchise:** A business model where a franchisee purchases the rights to operate under the established brand name, system, and products of a franchisor in exchange for fees and ongoing royalties.
* **Joint Venture:** An agreement between two or more independent businesses to work together on a specific commercial project or enterprise while maintaining their separate corporate identities.
* **Cooperative:** An organization owned and run democratically by its members (workers, consumers, or producers) to meet shared economic needs, with profits shared among members based on participation.

### 2. Public vs. Private Sector and Privatisation
* **Private Sector:** Businesses owned and run by private individuals and companies, primarily driven by profit and market share.
* **Public Sector:** Organizations owned and run by the national or local government, focused on providing essential public services, social welfare, and economic infrastructure.
* **Privatisation:** The transfer of ownership, control, or management of a business or service from the state (public sector) to private sector commercial owners.
  * *Arguments For:* Greater operational efficiency, profit-driven incentives, reduction in public spending.
  * *Arguments Against:* Risk of price increases for essential services, potential job cuts, neglect of social welfare priorities.
* **Nationalisation:** The purchase or transfer of private sector companies or assets into government ownership and control.
